# Screen Printing vs. Embroidery: Which Is Better for Corporate Apparel?

Screen printing and embroidery both put logos on garments. They just do it in fundamentally different ways, with different tradeoffs in cost, durability, and final look. Ordering corporate apparel for a 50-person team or a 500-person field crew? The wrong choice means wasted budget or shirts that look wrecked after three washes.

## Durability

Screen printing lays plastisol or water-based ink on top of the fabric. It bonds to the fibers but sits on the surface. Over time, especially with cotton-heavy garments washed in hot water, that layer cracks and peels. A properly cured screen print on a 50/50 poly-cotton blend holds up for 50 to 100 washes before noticeable fading. On 100% cotton, cracking starts sooner. I’ve seen it happen by wash 30 on cheap Gildan tees.

Embroidery punches thread directly through the fabric. The design is stitched in, not printed on. It doesn’t crack. It can fray if the thread quality is low or the digitizing is poor, but a well-done embroidery job on a structured cap or a polo will outlast the garment itself. The limitation: dense stitching can distort lightweight fabrics. A 10,000-stitch logo on a thin performance polo, say a Nike Dri-FIT, can pucker the material around the edges. That’s not a durability problem, it’s a look problem.

## Cost Per Unit

Screen printing wins on volume. Setup involves making a screen per color. A one-color logo on 100 shirts runs about $4 to $6 per shirt. Add a second color and you’re at $6 to $8. At 500 shirts, that per-unit price can drop to $2.50 or $3. The setup cost is fixed, so the more you print, the cheaper each shirt gets.

Embroidery has a digitizing fee per design, typically $30 to $75 depending on complexity and stitch count. Then each garment gets a per-piece charge. For a left-chest logo on a polo, expect $7 to $12 per shirt at low quantities (24 to 48 pieces). At 500, it might drop to $5 or $6. Embroidery never gets as cheap as screen printing at scale. The thread and machine time floor is higher.

## Design Complexity

Screen printing can reproduce photographic detail, gradients, and fine lines. A CMYK process print can render a full-color logo with shading. The catch: each color needs a separate screen, and registration gets harder with more colors. A six-color print is doable but costs more in setup and has a higher reject rate.

Embroidery is limited by stitch density and thread colors. You get solid fills, no gradients. Small text below 0.25 inches becomes a blurry mess. Intricate logos with thin lines or tiny details need to be simplified or they turn into a dense thread blob. A good digitizer can reduce a 12-color logo to 4 thread colors and still keep it readable. That’s a skill, not a machine setting.

## Perceived Value

Embroidery looks premium. A stitched logo on a polo or a jacket signals permanence and investment. Hospitality uniforms, executive gifts, and trade show staff wear benefit from that perception. Screen printing looks casual. It’s the right choice for T-shirts, event giveaways, and construction workwear where the garment will get dirty and replaced.

You can split the middle. A left-chest screen print on a high-quality polo can look sharp if the ink is opaque and the garment is dark. But in a direct comparison, most people reach for the embroidered polo first.

## Decision Criteria

**Garment type matters.** For polo shirts, embroidery is the standard. The collar and placket create a structured canvas, and a stitched logo complements that structure. For T-shirts and hoodies, screen printing is the default. For workwear like canvas jackets or heavy cotton coveralls, screen printing holds up better than embroidery because the thick fabric can distort dense stitching. For baseball caps, embroidery is the only serious option.

**Logo detail dictates the method.** A simple text-based logo or a solid shape works in both. A logo with small text, gradients, or fine lines forces you into screen printing or a simplified embroidery version. If the logo has a 6-point font tagline, don’t embroider it.

**Quantity pushes the cost inflection point.** Under 48 pieces, embroidery often costs the same or less than screen printing for a single-color logo. Over 100 pieces, screen printing pulls ahead on per-unit cost. Over 500, screen printing is dramatically cheaper.

One honest caveat: screen printing on polyester-rich fabrics requires special low-bleed inks. Cheap polyesters release dye when heat-cured, turning white ink pink. Not all print shops handle this well. Embroidery doesn’t have that problem. If your corporate apparel is all poly performance fabric, embroidery might be the safer bet even if the quantity favors screen printing.

For hospitality uniforms, the decision often splits. Front-of-house staff get embroidered polo shirts for the polished look. Back-of-house kitchen staff get screen-printed T-shirts or aprons because they’re replaced more frequently and the cost matters. That’s a practical split, not a compromise.
