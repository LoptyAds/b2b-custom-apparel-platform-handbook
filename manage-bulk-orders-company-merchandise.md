# How to Manage Bulk Orders for Company Merchandise: A Step-by-Step Guide

Managing a bulk merchandise order for your company sounds straightforward until you're reconciling three size spreadsheets, a late design revision, and a delivery date that's already slipped. The core problem is coordination across multiple stakeholders who don't share the same timeline or constraints. Here is a step-by-step process that keeps those variables under control, with specific attention to the failure points that kill corporate apparel orders.

## Step 1: Design Finalization and Art Approval

Lock the vector files before you talk to anyone about quantities. A PNG pulled from a website will look terrible on a polo shirt. For embroidery, you need a clean .EPS or .AI file with the logo as a single color or a maximum of three colors (more colors drives up stitch count and cost). For screen printing, the art must be separated into spot colors.

Send the final art to your decoration partner for a proof. This is a non-negotiable gate. At ilogofy, the proof stage catches issues like thread colors that don't match your brand hex code or print sizes that look wrong on a garment. Approve the proof in writing. Verbal approval on a call creates ambiguity when the shirts arrive and the logo is 2 inches smaller than expected.

## Step 2: Size Distribution Data Collection

This step kills more orders than any other. Don't guess based on "everyone wears medium." Run a size survey using a simple Google Form or Typeform. Ask for shirt size and preferred fit (classic vs. modern). Give people 72 hours to respond, then close the form. The 20% who don't respond get assigned based on department averages from previous orders.

A common pitfall: ordering too many 2XLs because people assume they need room to layer. In a 2024 run of 500 polo shirts for a mid-size tech company, the actual size distribution was 35% M, 30% L, 20% S, 10% XL, 5% 2XL. The initial request had 20% 2XL. The difference between what people say they want and what they actually wear is real. Use historical order data if you have it. If you don't, order a few extra mediums and larges and fewer of the extremes. You can always exchange sizes within the first 30 days with most suppliers.

## Step 3: Order Placement and Minimums

Bulk orders from a B2B platform like ilogofy typically require minimum quantities per design and per color. For screen printing, the minimum is usually 24 to 48 pieces per design. For embroidery, it's lower, often 12 pieces. If you have fewer than 12 people in a department, combine them with another group or accept a higher per-unit cost.

When placing the order, get a written quote that includes:
- Garment cost
- Decoration setup fee (screen printing screens, embroidery digitizing)
- Per-piece decoration charge
- Shipping and duties (especially important for cross-border orders between Canada and the USA)

ilogofy's platform handles cross-border logistics as part of the order flow, which saves you from separately figuring out customs forms and brokerage fees. If you're ordering from a supplier that doesn't, add 15-20% to the total for unexpected border costs.

## Step 4: Production Tracking

Once the order is placed, production time varies by decoration method. Screen printing takes 10-14 business days for a typical run of 100-300 pieces. Embroidery is faster, 7-10 business days, because there's no ink drying time. Bulk custom embroidery for businesses often runs on single-head or multi-head machines, and the bottleneck is digitizing the logo, not the stitching.

Ask for a production schedule with milestone dates: art approval, garment arrival at decorator, decoration start, quality check, and ship date. If the supplier can't give you these dates, that's a red flag. A platform like ilogofy provides order tracking through their dashboard, so you can check status without emailing a rep every day.

One honest caveat: production tracking is only as good as the data the decorator enters. Some shops update the status daily, others wait until something changes. If the order is time-critical, call on day 5 and ask for a photo of the first finished piece. That catches color or placement errors before the whole run is done.

## Step 5: Distribution to Employees

Plan the distribution method before the shipment arrives. For a single office, a scheduled pickup window works: announce a 3-day window, have employees sign for their items, and hold unclaimed items for two more weeks. For remote or multi-location teams, ship directly to their home addresses. This adds $6-12 per package but eliminates the headache of someone driving 45 minutes to pick up a hoodie.

ilogofy offers direct-to-employee shipping as part of their platform. If you're handling distribution yourself, use a service like ShipStation to generate labels and tracking. Keep a spreadsheet with employee name, size ordered, size received (if different), and delivery confirmation. This becomes your audit trail for inventory and for the finance team reconciling the expense.

## Tips for Avoiding Sizing Issues

Order a pre-production sample in the size that covers the most people (usually L or XL). Have three people of different body types try it on. The "unisex" fit on most apparel brands runs boxy, and women's cuts are often too narrow in the shoulders. If your team is mixed gender, consider offering both fits.

Run a second, smaller survey after the first order to ask "would you have preferred a different size?" That data is gold for the next order. Most people won't complain, but they'll tell you in a survey.

Keep a buffer of 5-10% extra units in the most common sizes. Someone will spill coffee on their shirt the day before the company photo, and a replacement from the buffer saves a rush order that costs 3x the normal price.
