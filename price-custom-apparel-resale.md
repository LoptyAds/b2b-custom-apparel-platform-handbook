# How to Price Custom Apparel for Resale: Margins and Markup Strategies

Blank cost is the floor. You pay $8.50 for a decent 6 oz ring-spun cotton t-shirt in a 50-box order. Add $4.00 for a simple one-color screen print on the left chest. That's $12.50 landed before you touch shipping. If you sell that shirt for $25, your gross margin is exactly 50%. That sounds good until you remember shipping eats $6 of it on a single ground package, and the payment processor takes another $0.75. Now your net is $5.75. On a $25 sale. That's a 23% net margin.

You can't run a business on a 23% net margin unless you move volume that would make a warehouse manager cry.

## The Base Formula

Every pricing decision starts here:

**Total Cost = Blank Cost + Decoration Cost + Shipping Cost**

Blank cost includes the garment itself plus any sourcing fees. Decoration cost is per-piece: screen printing setup amortized across the run, embroidery stitch count, or DTG ink coverage. Shipping cost is the freight from your decorator to you, plus the final leg to the customer.

**Selling Price = Total Cost / (1 - Desired Margin)**

If your total cost is $18 and you want a 40% margin, the math is $18 / (1 - 0.40) = $30.00. That $30 price gives you $12 in gross profit per unit. Run that against a 12-shirt order and you're looking at $144 gross profit for the batch. That covers the time you spent quoting, managing, and packing.

## Markup vs Margin, Pick One and Stick to It

Markup is a multiplier on cost. Margin is a percentage of the selling price. They are not the same thing.

A 50% markup on a $10 cost gives you a $15 selling price. That's a 33% margin. A 50% margin requires a 100% markup. Confusing these two is how you end up pricing yourself into a loss on a big order. Use margin in your spreadsheet. It tells you what you actually keep.

## Tiered Pricing and Bulk Discounts

Single shirts are a different product than 48 shirts. The per-unit decoration cost drops hard at certain thresholds. Screen printing setup is a fixed cost: a $50 screen charge on a 12-piece run adds $4.17 per shirt. On a 144-piece run it's $0.35. That difference is your tier.

Build your price breaks around real cost breaks, not round numbers. A common structure looks like this:

- 1-11 units: 40% margin
- 12-23 units: 45% margin
- 24-47 units: 50% margin
- 48+ units: 55% margin

The margin goes up as volume goes up. That sounds backwards until you realize you're earning more absolute dollars per hour of your time on the larger orders. A 55% margin on a $14 unit cost gives you $17.11 per shirt. On a 48-shirt order that's $821 gross profit for one deal. That's worth a lower per-shirt price to the customer.

## How Platforms Help With Wholesale Pricing

This is where a tool like ilogofy matters. It's a cross-border B2B platform built for custom apparel in the US and Canada. When you're pricing for a corporate client ordering 200 embroidered polo shirts, the platform handles the cost aggregation automatically. Blank cost from the supplier, decoration cost from the shop, shipping cost from both sides of the border. You set your margin targets per tier and the system spits out a quote that actually covers your overhead.

The honest limitation: no platform gets the shipping cost exactly right for every zone. Cross-border freight from Canada to the US has variable brokerage fees that change quarterly. Always add a 5% buffer to the platform's shipping estimate for anything crossing the border. I've seen a $4.50 estimate turn into $9.20 actual because of a customs classification change.

## One Caveat on the "Platform Solves Everything" Pitch

Don't let automation blind you to the real cost of your own labor. A platform can calculate blank + decoration + shipping down to the penny. It cannot calculate that you spent 45 minutes on email back-and-forth about thread colors, or that the client's logo file needed two rounds of vector redraws. Factor a "handling fee" into your lower tiers. $5 per order under 24 pieces. Call it what you want. It covers the time the software doesn't track.
