# Cross-Border Logistics & Corporate Branding Use Cases

## The Geography Problem in Apparel Decoration

Cross-border shipping in custom apparel is not a logistics detail. It is a structural constraint that shapes how B2B decoration platforms must handle pricing, tariff classification, and delivery timelines. The US-Canada corridor looks simple on a map. In practice, it introduces variables that most domestic-only decorators never need to think about.

Consider a US-based hospitality chain that needs 500 branded polo shirts for a new hotel opening in Toronto. A domestic decorator in Chicago would handle the order, ship it to a US address, and stop there. When that shipment crosses the border, three things shift. The tariff classification of the garment determines duty rates. The origin of the blank garment matters for USMCA eligibility. Corporate branding can affect how customs classifies the shipment.

## Duty and Tariff Considerations

The US-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) governs most apparel trade between the three countries. A garment assembled in Mexico from US fabric qualifies for preferential treatment. A garment cut and sewn in China and only decorated in the US does not. Duty rates on apparel range from 0% to 32%. The wrong classification on a customs form can delay a shipment by weeks.

For corporate branded merchandise, tariff classification gets more specific. A polo shirt with a small embroidered logo is classified the same as a blank polo shirt. A shirt with a full back print, pocket embroidery, and sleeve patches may shift into a different category if the decoration is considered substantial enough to change the garment's character. Customs officers make these determinations case by case.

If the decoration covers less than 15 percent of the garment's surface area, it is unlikely to change the tariff classification. Full-coverage screen prints or multiple decoration types on a single garment should be reviewed by a customs broker before the order is placed.

## US-to-Canada Workflow

A typical US-to-Canada branded apparel order follows this path. The buyer selects garments from US inventory. Decoration happens at a US facility. The finished goods are shipped to a Canadian address. At the border, the shipment clears customs with a commercial invoice, the correct HS code, and a USMCA certificate of origin if applicable.

The buyer's risk is that the shipment gets held for documentation issues. Missing a country of origin statement or using the wrong six-digit HS code can trigger a physical inspection. That inspection adds five to ten business days. For a hotel opening or a trade show, that delay is a failure.

## Canada-to-US Workflow

The reverse direction has its own complications. Canadian decorators often source blank garments from US suppliers, which means the raw materials cross the border twice: first as blanks, then as finished goods. Duty is owed on the value added by decoration, not on the full value of the garment. Proving that value requires itemized invoices that separate the cost of the blank from the cost of decoration.

Canadian businesses shipping to US customers also face the de minimis threshold. Shipments valued under $800 USD enter duty-free. Above that threshold, the importer must file a formal entry and pay applicable duties. A single order of 200 custom hoodies at $40 each exceeds that threshold easily.

## Corporate Branded Apparel Use Cases

### Hospitality Uniforms

Hotels and restaurants need uniforms that match across properties. A US-based hotel group with Canadian locations needs the same garment, same decoration, and same quality standard on both sides of the border. Canadian distributors may carry different blank garment brands or colors than their US counterparts. A color in stock in Chicago may be backordered in Vancouver for six weeks. Cross-border platforms that maintain consistent inventory across both countries solve this directly.

### Construction Workwear

Workwear branding is not primarily about aesthetics. Hard hats, high-visibility vests, and flame-resistant coveralls must meet safety standards that differ between the US and Canada. CSA and ANSI certifications are not interchangeable. A branded vest that passes US standards may not be legal for use on a Canadian job site. The decorator must verify that the blank garment carries the correct certification for the destination country before any decoration work begins.

### Promotional Merchandise

Trade show giveaways and corporate gifts are typically small orders: fifty hats, a hundred T-shirts, two hundred tote bags. The unit economics of cross-border shipping punish small orders. A $10 T-shirt can cost $18 to ship across the border if the order is too small to qualify for consolidated freight. Platforms that aggregate multiple small orders into a single shipment reduce this friction.

### Custom Patches and Labels

Patches are not garments. They are decorative accessories classified under HS code 5810.10 for embroidered badges. They ship easily in small envelopes and rarely trigger duty. But they require a separate production run from the garments they will be attached to. Coordinating the timeline so that patches and garments arrive at the same location on the same day is a project management problem, not a decoration problem.
