# How to Order Custom Workwear with Logos for Construction Teams

Ordering custom workwear for construction crews is not the same as buying company t-shirts for a picnic. The fabric has to survive abrasion, repeated washing, and exposure to sun and dirt. The logos have to stay legible after all of that. And the garments themselves must meet OSHA or ANSI standards for high visibility and flame resistance depending on the job site. Skip any of those constraints, and the gear fails on safety or durability, wasting money on replacements.

**Fabric selection for construction environments** starts with weave and weight. Cotton duck canvas (12 oz or heavier) is standard for trades like framing and roofing because it stands up to snags and holds its shape. For teams that work outdoors in heat, a cotton/polyester blend with moisture wicking properties dries faster and breathes. Avoid 100% polyester for high heat tasks near welding or open flame unless the fabric is specifically FR rated, polyester melts rather than chars, creating a burn risk.

For cold weather, look for lined jackets or hooded sweatshirts with a brushed interior. Carhartt and Dickies are common baseline brands, but many suppliers (including platforms like ilogofy) also carry house brands that meet the same weight specs at lower unit cost. Demand fabric weight in ounces per square yard: below 8 oz is too light for daily construction use, while 10 oz to 14 oz is the sweet spot.

**High visibility standards and safety compliance** hinge on ANSI 107. It defines three performance classes. Class 2 is the minimum for most road construction and utility work. Class 3 is required for high speed traffic or low light conditions. Your supplier must provide garments that carry the ANSI 107 label, not just a reflective strip sewn on. The background fabric color must meet specific chromaticity and luminance requirements. Fluorescent yellow-green, orange-red, or red are the only acceptable background colors under the standard.

Flame resistant (FR) gear adds another layer. NFPA 70E governs arc flash protection. ASTM F1506 covers FR fabrics for electrical workers. If your team works near energized lines or welding, do not skip the FR requirement. Embroidery on FR garments is possible, but the thread must be Nomex or similar FR rated thread, standard polyester thread can melt and drip into the fabric, creating a secondary burn hazard.

**Logo placement for maximum visibility** matters differently on each item. On a high visibility vest or jacket, the best spot is the left chest or the upper back between the shoulder blades. Chest placement keeps the logo visible when the worker faces a customer or inspector. Back placement is readable from a distance when the worker is turned away, common on active sites. Avoid placing logos on the reflective tape itself, the tape is engineered for retroreflection, and embroidery or screen printing on top degrades that property.

For hard hats, use adhesive vinyl decals or custom patches with peel-and-stick backing. Embroidery on the front of a hard hat is not practical, the needle can compromise the shell. Patches sewn onto the brim or sides of a cap work fine, but keep them small, no more than 3 inches across, to avoid interfering with the hat's fit.

**Embroidery vs. screen printing for workwear** comes down to abuse tolerance. Embroidery stitched directly into the fabric does not peel, crack, or fade as fast as screen printed ink. A 10,000 stitch logo on a denim shirt will still look clean after 50 wash cycles. Screen printing on a heavy cotton shirt starts to crack around the 20 wash mark, especially if the dryer runs hot.

Cost and detail flip the equation. Embroidery has a higher setup fee, digitizing the logo into a stitch file, usually $20 to $60 per design, and a per piece charge that scales with stitch count. A simple 5,000 stitch logo might cost $3 to $5 per piece. A dense 15,000 stitch logo can run $8 to $12 per piece. Screen printing has a lower per piece cost at high volumes, often under $2 per print for a one-color logo on 50 shirts. But for workwear that sees real abuse, the lower upfront cost is not worth the replacement cycle.

One caveat: embroidery on very lightweight fabrics (thin cotton t-shirts, performance mesh) can pucker or leave needle holes that weaken the fabric. On those items, screen printing or heat transfer vinyl is the safer choice. Most construction workwear is heavy enough to handle embroidery without issue.

**Bulk pricing and order strategy** shift at specific volume thresholds. The breakpoint for meaningful per-piece savings is usually 24 units. Below that, you pay full setup fees per design and per garment. At 50 to 100 units, most suppliers drop the per-piece price by 15% to 25%. At 200+, you can negotiate custom fabric colors or private label tags.

Order in batches by size range, not by individual name. Construction teams have high turnover, so ordering 20 large shirts and 20 extra larges is more practical than ordering one of each size per person. Keep a buffer of 10% to 15% over the headcount for replacements.
