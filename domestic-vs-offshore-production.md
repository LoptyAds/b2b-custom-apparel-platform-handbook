# How to Choose Between Domestic and Offshore Production for Custom Apparel

The decision between domestic and offshore production for custom apparel is rarely a clean binary. It depends on order size, SKU complexity, branding requirements, and how much tolerance your team has for email threads that span time zones. For a B2B platform like [ilogofy](https://www.ilogofy.com), which routes orders across the US and Canada and coordinates with overseas facilities, the question is not which country wins. It is which production node fits the specific job.

## The Cost Curve Flattens, Then Inverts

Offshore production wins on unit price for large runs. A 500-piece order of embroidered polos might cost 30 to 40 percent less per unit in Vietnam or Bangladesh than in a US shop, before freight and duties. But that gap narrows fast as order size drops. Below 50 units, the per-piece savings often vanish entirely because offshore minimums force you to overproduce, and the shipping cost per garment climbs sharply.

Domestic production has a different curve. Higher base labor rates, but no ocean freight, no customs brokerage, no 30-day lead time for a container. For a 20-piece order of custom polo shirts for a small sales team, a domestic shop can often deliver in 7 to 10 days at a price that beats offshore once you factor in expedited shipping and the cost of correcting a wrong size run. The crossover point, based on orders routed through [ilogofy](https://www.ilogofy.com), sits around 100 to 150 units for simple screen printing, and higher for embroidery where setup costs dominate.

## Lead Time Is a Hard Constraint, Not a Preference

Offshore lead times are structural. You are looking at 4 to 6 weeks for production alone, plus 2 to 3 weeks for ocean freight, plus customs clearance which is unpredictable. Air freight collapses the shipping window to 5 days but can add $4 to $8 per garment, eating the entire labor cost advantage.

Domestic lead times are shorter but not instant. A US or Canadian embroidery shop typically quotes 5 to 10 business days for a run of 50 to 200 pieces, assuming the artwork is print-ready and the blanks are in stock. The real risk with domestic is not speed, it is capacity. During peak seasons (Q4 corporate gifts, trade show season in spring), domestic shops book out. You can wait three weeks for a domestic order in October that would take two weeks in February.

The trade-off is not domestic speed versus offshore slowness. It is predictability versus flexibility. Offshore gives you a fixed calendar if you plan ahead. Domestic gives you the ability to react to a last-minute rebrand or a delayed product launch.

## Quality Control: Proximity Buys You a Phone Call

Quality issues happen everywhere. The difference is how fast you learn about them and what it costs to fix them.

With domestic production, you can request a physical sample before the full run, approve it, and then do a press check in person if the shop is within driving distance. If a batch of construction workwear comes back with a misaligned logo, you can ship it back and have a redo in a week. The financial exposure is capped at the order value plus some shipping.

Offshore, the sample approval process is the only real control point. You are approving a digital proof or a physical sample that takes 5 days to arrive. Once the production run starts, you are not seeing the goods until they are on a boat. A color shift on a screen print that looks acceptable on a monitor can be a disaster on fabric. And rework means another 4-week production cycle, not a phone call.

Offshore quality is not inherently worse. Many overseas factories specialize in embroidery and screen printing and produce work that matches or exceeds mid-tier US shops. The issue is variance. You might get a flawless run from a Vietnamese factory three times in a row, then a fourth run with loose threads on every patch because the operator changed. Domestic shops have the same variance, but you can stand over them while they fix it.

## Communication Is the Hidden Tax

The most underestimated cost in offshore production is not freight. It is the back-and-forth on specs. Time zone differences mean a question from your team at 2 PM gets answered at 9 PM the same day, if you are lucky. More often, it waits until the next morning. A simple clarification on thread color or placement can stretch into two days.

Domestic communication is synchronous. You call, you get an answer, you move on. For a run of 20 custom embroidered hats, that speed matters. For a 2,000-piece hospitality uniform order, the extra days of asynchronous communication are amortized across a much larger project and become negligible.

## Order Size Drives the Decision Matrix

Here is a rough heuristic that holds up in practice:

- **1 to 50 units:** Domestic, always. The offshore price advantage is noise at this volume.
- **50 to 200 units:** Domestic unless you have a long lead time and a very standardized product (plain color, one logo, no size customization). Test the offshore waters with a low-risk order.
- **200 to 1,000 units:** This is the gray zone. Domestic wins if you need speed or have complex branding (multiple logo placements, custom patches, special threads). Offshore wins if the product is simple and you can wait.
- **1,000+ units:** Offshore is usually the right call for unit economics, but only if your quality tolerance is flexible and you have a contract that defines defect rates.

One caveat: these thresholds shift with product type. Custom patches and labels for uniforms are small, high-value items that travel cheaply and are easy to inspect. They are a good offshore candidate even at lower volumes. Bulk screen printing on t-shirts is bulky and heavy, so the freight cost punishes offshore at lower volumes.

## The Rise of Multi-Country Orchestration

The newer development is not offshore versus domestic. It is hybrid. Platforms now split a single order across production nodes based on the item. A corporate apparel order might have 300 polos embroidered in the US for the sales team that needs them in two weeks, while 2,000 construction workwear shirts with reflective striping and logo printing go to a factory in Pakistan with a 6-week lead time.

This is not just cost arbitrage. It is risk spreading. If a domestic shop has a machine breakdown, the offshore portion of the order is unaffected. If a port strike delays the offshore container, the domestic portion still ships on time. The downside is complexity. You now manage two production schedules, two sets of quality standards, and two shipping legs. That is where a platform like [ilogofy](https://www.ilogofy.com) earns its keep, by acting as the single interface while the actual work happens in multiple countries.

The honest limitation here is that this model only works if your order is large enough to split meaningfully. Splitting a 100-piece order across two countries doubles your setup costs and your chances of a mistake. The hybrid approach is a tool for mid-to-large B2B buyers, not for a startup ordering 40 branded hoodies.

## What Actually Decides the Choice

Forget the cost spreadsheet for a second. The real question is: what is the cost of being wrong? If you order offshore and the quality is off, you are out the full order value plus the time to reorder. If you order domestic and the quality is off, you are out a week and a return shipping label. For promotional apparel Canada that is going to a trade show in 10 days, the domestic option is not a choice, it is the only option. For a warehouse uniform program that will last 18 months, offshore makes sense even with a 5 percent defect rate, because you can order 10 percent extra inventory to cover it.

The space is evolving toward platforms that abstract away the geography. The buyer does not need to know whether the embroidery machine is in Ohio or Guangdong. They need to know the delivery date, the unit cost, and the defect tolerance. That is the value of a cross-border platform.
