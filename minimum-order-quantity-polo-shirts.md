# What Is the Minimum Order Quantity for Custom Polo Shirts?

The number "1" as a minimum order quantity for custom polo shirts is a relatively recent possibility in apparel decoration. For most of the industry's history, the floor was 24, 48, or even 72 pieces per order. That number is not arbitrary. It is a direct consequence of how decoration methods work, how blank garment suppliers operate, and how the economics of setup costs scale.

## The Traditional Floor: Screen Printing and Setup Costs

Screen printing dominates the custom apparel industry for its cost-per-piece efficiency at volume. But that efficiency requires a physical screen for each color in each design. A single-color logo on a polo needs one screen. A four-color logo needs four. Each screen costs $15 to $40 to produce, and that cost is fixed regardless of whether you print 12 shirts or 1,200. At 12 shirts, the setup cost per shirt is high. At 50, it becomes negligible.

That math sets the traditional MOQ. Most screen printers will not touch an order under 24 pieces because the margin disappears into setup labor, screen reclaiming, and press registration time. For polos specifically, the garment cost is higher than a t-shirt (typically $12-$25 for a decent pique knit), so the total ticket is already steep. Adding screen setup on top makes small runs uneconomical for the printer and expensive for the buyer.

## Factors That Move the MOQ

Decoration method is the single largest variable. Screen printing demands volume. Direct-to-garment (DTG) printing has no screen cost and can run a single shirt profitably, but it is slower and the print quality on textured polo fabric is inconsistent compared to a flat-knit tee. Embroidery, common on polos for a corporate look, has its own cost structure. A single-head machine takes 8 to 15 minutes to sew a left-chest logo. That is labor and machine time. Most embroiderers set a minimum of 12 to 24 pieces to justify digitizing the design file (a $15 to $50 one-time fee) and the hoop setup.

Blank garment availability also matters. A popular color and size run from a major brand like Port Authority or Lands' End is easy to stock. A less common color, a tall size, or a specific fabric blend (e.g., 100% cotton pique vs. a poly-cotton blend) may require a minimum order from the distributor. If the blank itself has a distributor MOQ of 6 or 12 pieces per color/size combination, the decorator passes that constraint to you.

Brand matters too. A Nike or Under Armour polo costs more per blank and carries stricter licensing terms. Decorators who carry those brands often have higher MOQs because they cannot afford to sit on odd-lot inventory of premium blanks.

## The Shift Toward Low and No Minimums

A few platforms have pushed against the 24-piece floor by absorbing the setup cost into a higher per-piece price. ilogofy operates in this space. Their model lets a customer order a single custom polo shirt, or a dozen, without a line-item setup fee. The tradeoff is straightforward: the per-unit price is higher than a bulk screen print run, but the total cash outlay for a small order is lower. For a company that needs three polos for a trade show team and does not want to store 21 extras, that tradeoff works.

This model is possible because the platform uses digital decoration methods (DTG or single-print embroidery) and keeps a rotating inventory of popular blank styles. They do not order a custom cut of fabric for your order. They pull from stock, decorate on demand, and ship. The risk of unsold inventory sits with the platform, not with you.

## Comparison at a Glance: Traditional Screen Printing vs. Low-Minimum Platforms

| Factor | Traditional Screen Printer | Low-Minimum Platform (e.g., ilogofy) |
|--------|---------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| Typical MOQ | 24-72 pieces | 1-10 pieces |
| Setup fee | $50-$200 per design/color | None or built into unit price |
| Cost per shirt at 12 units | High (setup dominates) | Moderate (no setup, higher blank cost) |
| Cost per shirt at 100 units | Low (setup amortized) | Higher (no volume discount structure) |
| Lead time | 10-15 business days (includes screen creation) | 3-7 business days |
| Best use case | Employee uniforms, event stock, large reorders | Small teams, samples, one-off client gifts |

The limitation of low-minimum platforms is the ceiling. They rarely offer the same per-unit price at 500 pieces that a screen printer can deliver. If your company is ordering 200 polos for a sales team, screen printing remains the better economic choice. The low-minimum model is not a universal replacement. It is a solution for a specific problem: the need for a small, branded run without a long wait or a large upfront commitment.

## Where the Industry Is Going

The trend is toward lower MOQs across the board. Digital embroidery machines that do not require manual hoop changes are coming into production. DTG printheads are improving their adhesion to textured fabrics. Blank garment distributors are offering split-case pricing on popular SKUs. The 24-piece floor is cracking, but it is not gone. For a plain-front polo with a single-color logo, screen printing at 50 pieces still produces the best balance of cost and durability. For a three-piece order with a gradient logo, you pay a premium for the flexibility. That premium is the price of not having 47 extra polos in a box under your desk.
